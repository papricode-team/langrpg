## The verb is your anchor

In an ordinary German main-clause statement, the conjugated verb goes in the second position. That means the second **sentence element**, not necessarily the second word. A whole phrase can occupy the first position.

Compare <span lang="de">Ich trinke heute Kaffee</span> (“I’m drinking coffee today”) and <span lang="de">Heute trinke ich Kaffee</span> (“Today I’m drinking coffee”). The emphasis changes, but <span lang="de">trinke</span> stays in the second position. When <span lang="de">heute</span> comes first, the subject moves after the verb.

| First element | Conjugated verb | The rest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="de">Ich</span> | <span lang="de">lerne</span> | <span lang="de">Deutsch.</span> |
| <span lang="de">Am Morgen</span> | <span lang="de">lerne</span> | <span lang="de">ich Deutsch.</span> |
| <span lang="de">In diesem Café</span> | <span lang="de">trinke</span> | <span lang="de">ich Tee.</span> |

<span lang="de">In diesem Café</span> contains three words but is one element. Counting words would put the verb in the wrong place; looking for the pieces of the sentence works better.

## Questions have two common shapes

For a yes-or-no question, put the conjugated verb first: <span lang="de">Trinkst du Kaffee?</span> (“Do you drink coffee?”). English often adds “do”; German does not need an extra equivalent here.

For a question with a question word, start with that word or phrase, then the verb: <span lang="de">Wo wohnst du?</span> (“Where do you live?”) or <span lang="de">Wann kommt der Zug?</span> (“When does the train arrive?”).

Practise turning one statement into both kinds of question. <span lang="de">Du wohnst in Berlin</span> becomes <span lang="de">Wohnst du in Berlin?</span> and <span lang="de">Wo wohnst du?</span> The repeated material lets you focus on the change in order.

## Two verb parts can frame a sentence

With a modal verb such as <span lang="de">können</span>, the modal is conjugated and the other verb stays in the infinitive at the end: <span lang="de">Ich kann heute Deutsch üben</span> (“I can practise German today”).

Separable verbs create another frame. <span lang="de">Ankommen</span> means “to arrive,” but a simple main clause can be <span lang="de">Der Zug kommt um acht Uhr an</span> (“The train arrives at eight o’clock”). The prefix <span lang="de">an</span> moves to the end.

The conversational past tense with <span lang="de">haben</span> or <span lang="de">sein</span> has a similar shape: <span lang="de">Ich habe einen Kaffee bestellt</span> (“I ordered a coffee”). The conjugated auxiliary comes second; the past participle comes at the end.

## Give a reason with weil

<span lang="de">Weil</span> means “because.” In a standard written subordinate clause introduced by <span lang="de">weil</span>, the conjugated verb goes to the end: <span lang="de">Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Berlin wohne.</span> (“I’m learning German because I live in Berlin.”)

Keep the comma between the main and subordinate clauses. Begin with a short reason and only add more detail when the basic order feels manageable. You may hear different structures in informal speech, but the verb-final pattern is the reliable starting point for standard written German.

## Build three sentences yourself

1. Start <span lang="de">Ich trinke heute Tee</span> with <span lang="de">Heute</span>.
2. Turn <span lang="de">Du lernst Deutsch</span> into a yes-or-no question.
3. Finish: <span lang="de">Ich bleibe hier, weil …</span> using <span lang="de">ich / Zeit / habe</span>.

Do not try to solve all of German word order at once. Learn one pattern, use it with a few different words, then return to it in a [short review](/learn-german/german-vocabulary-practice/).
