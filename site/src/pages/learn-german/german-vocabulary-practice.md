## Remember a word you can use

A useful German vocabulary note contains more than a translation. For a noun, include its article and, when helpful, its plural. Add one short sentence that makes the meaning clear. <span lang="de">Der Schlüssel. Ich finde den Schlüssel.</span> gives “key” a role in an action you can imagine.

For a verb, learn a small sentence as well as the dictionary form. <span lang="de">Ich brauche Hilfe</span> (“I need help”) shows how <span lang="de">brauchen</span> works with something you need. Later, change the object: <span lang="de">Ich brauche einen Stift</span> (“I need a pen”).

Choose words connected to your next activity. A café visit, travel plan or story scene gives you a manageable group. You do not need to collect every unfamiliar word you encounter.

## Separate recognition from recall

Recognition is seeing <span lang="de">der Schlüssel</span> and knowing that it means “the key.” Recall is starting with the idea of a key and producing the German yourself. Listening adds another task: recognising the word when you hear it rather than see it.

Practise in more than one direction. Read the German and give the meaning; then hide it and produce the German. If you know the meaning but forget the article, record that specific gap rather than declaring the whole word either learned or lost.

Avoid judging your knowledge immediately after rereading. The answer may still be sitting in short-term memory. Returning later gives you a more informative test of what you can retrieve.

## Space the returns

Spaced practice means returning to material across separate sessions instead of putting every repetition into one sitting. Retrieval practice means attempting an answer from memory before checking it. Research has found benefits from both, although the best timing depends on the material and how long you want to retain it.

A practical starting routine is to revisit new phrases on the next day, then after a few more days, and again later if they remain useful. This is an example schedule, not a universal formula. Missed or hesitant answers deserve an earlier return; reliable answers can wait longer.

Always check after an attempt. Repeating a mistaken form without feedback can make it familiar too. Keep corrections specific: article, spelling, meaning or sentence shape.

## A ten-minute vocabulary visit

1. **Recall for three minutes.** Cover yesterday’s German phrases and try them from the meanings or situations.
2. **Check for two minutes.** Correct errors and say or write the complete phrase once.
3. **Use for three minutes.** Make a fresh sentence with two of the words. Keep the grammar simple enough to check.
4. **Choose for two minutes.** Pick a few useful phrases from your next conversation or scene. Stop before the list becomes a burden.

The timings are flexible. If you have five minutes, choose fewer words. If your review pile keeps growing, pause new vocabulary and work on what is already there.

## Make the next encounter different

Suppose you learned <span lang="de">die Rechnung</span> (“the bill”) in a café. Next time, ask <span lang="de">Kann ich die Rechnung haben?</span> (“Can I have the bill?”). Later, listen for the word in a short restaurant exchange. Changing the task helps you notice whether you know the word beyond one familiar card.

Glotworld keeps separate evidence for different kinds of practice and uses spaced review for word memory. A familiar word may return in a review or world interaction. Your own small routine outside the game can give it another useful context.

## Try a recall check

Without looking above, how would you say “I need help”? Can you remember the article for “key”? Then make a question that could help you find it.
