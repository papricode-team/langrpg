## Learn the article with the noun

German nouns have grammatical gender. In the nominative singular, the definite articles are <span lang="de">der</span> for masculine nouns, <span lang="de">die</span> for feminine nouns and <span lang="de">das</span> for neuter nouns. All three can translate as “the.”

Instead of learning <span lang="de">Bahnhof</span> by itself, learn <span lang="de">der Bahnhof</span>. Instead of a card that says only “house,” write <span lang="de">das Haus</span>. Treating the article as part of the word is more useful than learning a bare noun and trying to attach its gender later.

| Article | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="de">der</span> | <span lang="de">der Bahnhof</span> | the railway station |
| <span lang="de">die</span> | <span lang="de">die Straße</span> | the street |
| <span lang="de">das</span> | <span lang="de">das Haus</span> | the house |

Grammatical gender is a property of the word. It is not a reliable description of the object or person. For example, <span lang="de">das Mädchen</span> (“the girl”) is grammatically neuter because the diminutive ending <span lang="de">-chen</span> makes the noun neuter.

## Useful patterns, with honest limits

Some endings are helpful clues. Nouns ending in <span lang="de">-ung</span>, <span lang="de">-heit</span> and <span lang="de">-keit</span> are feminine: <span lang="de">die Zeitung</span> (newspaper), <span lang="de">die Freiheit</span> (freedom), <span lang="de">die Möglichkeit</span> (possibility). Diminutives ending in <span lang="de">-chen</span> and <span lang="de">-lein</span> are neuter.

Days and months are masculine: <span lang="de">der Montag</span>, <span lang="de">der Oktober</span>. Many nouns ending in <span lang="de">-e</span> are feminine, such as <span lang="de">die Tasche</span> (bag), but this is only a tendency. Common exceptions include <span lang="de">der Name</span> (name) and <span lang="de">das Ende</span> (end).

Use patterns to make a better guess, then check a dictionary. A small set of reliable patterns is more useful than a giant list of rules you cannot recall in conversation.

## The plural has its own job

The definite article for nominative plural nouns is <span lang="de">die</span>, regardless of the singular gender: <span lang="de">der Bahnhof → die Bahnhöfe</span>, <span lang="de">die Straße → die Straßen</span>, <span lang="de">das Haus → die Häuser</span>.

That does not mean the singular gender changes. It means plural articles follow a different pattern. Add a plural to your vocabulary notes when the word is useful enough to revisit. German plurals are not formed by adding one universal ending.

## Why der sometimes becomes den

Articles also show case: the noun’s role in the sentence. Compare <span lang="de">Der Bahnhof ist hier</span> (“The station is here”) with <span lang="de">Ich sehe den Bahnhof</span> (“I see the station”). In the second sentence, the station is the direct object, so masculine <span lang="de">der</span> becomes <span lang="de">den</span>.

Start by learning the nominative article. Then practise a small number of meaningful sentence patterns, rather than trying to learn all article forms in one sitting. You will meet another useful change in [ordering coffee](/learn-german/ordering-coffee-in-german/): <span lang="de">ein Kaffee → einen Kaffee</span>.

## A five-minute practice routine

Choose five objects or places you care about. Write each noun with its article and a short example. Cover the German and recall the whole phrase from a picture or meaning. Check immediately, and revisit the ones you missed another day.

You can colour-code genders if you enjoy it, but still say or write the article: recognising a colour is not the same as retrieving the German word. Mix the order so you cannot answer from the position of the card.
